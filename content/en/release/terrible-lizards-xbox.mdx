## The Story: Dalton's Dinos, Route 66, 1992

Terrible Lizards takes place at Dalton's Dinos, a roadside dinosaur attraction off Route 66 in New Mexico. It was once the kind of stop families pulled over for — giant fiberglass reptiles, a gift shop, a parking lot baking in the desert sun. Now the staff are gone, the place is crumbling, and your car has died miles from anywhere.

It's 1992, which matters more than it sounds. No cell phones. No rideshares. No help coming. The one working phone you find rings with a voice begging you to locate a missing child, and that request is what keeps you walking deeper instead of running for the highway.

The setup does a lot of heavy lifting for the horror. Isolation is baked into the era, not bolted on. There's no radio call, no backup, no way to explain what you're seeing to anyone. Meanwhile, something is moving between the exhibits, and it is not a statue.

## Horror Meets Parkour: How Movement Works

The core idea behind Terrible Lizards is that combat isn't just discouraged — it's absent. The store description is blunt about it: there are no weapons except your wits. Everything you do is an escape, and every escape costs you a little distance you have to earn back.

That puts enormous weight on the movement system. The game describes fluid, momentum-driven traversal that's easy to pick up and hard to master, which is the classic skill ceiling you want in a chase game. If you've ever played a first-person parkour game and felt the difference between a clean line and a clumsy one, that's the feeling this leans into.

| Move | When You'll Use It | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Run | Open ground between exhibits | Builds the momentum every other move depends on |
| Jump | Gaps, ledges, debris | Cheapest way to break a pursuer's line |
| Climb | Fences, walls, ride structures | Removes you from the ground floor entirely |
| Wall run | Tight corridors and facades | Keeps speed up when a straight line ends |
| Bounce | Off surfaces mid-chase | Redirects momentum without stopping |
| Slide | Under obstacles and around corners | Fastest way to duck a charge |

### Why "No Weapons" Is a Design Choice, Not a Limitation

Removing combat does three things at once. First, it makes every encounter a movement puzzle instead of a damage check. Second, it guarantees tension — you can never "clear" a room, only survive it. Third, it keeps the pacing relentless, because the only verbs available to you are motion verbs.

Community reports from chase-horror fans tend to agree on one point: games like this live or die on whether the movement feels fair. When a death feels like your mistake, you reload immediately. When it feels like the game's fault, you put the controller down. That's the tightrope Terrible Lizards is walking, and it's the single biggest thing to watch in early reviews.
