## The Story: Dalton's Dinos and a Missing Child

Terrible Lizards takes place at Dalton's Dinos, a once-popular roadside attraction on Route 66 in New Mexico. The premise is deliberately retro: it's 1992, you have no cell phone, no rideshare app, and no way to call for help. Your car has died miles from anywhere, and the staff have vanished.

Then a telephone inside the attraction rings. A mysterious voice on the other end begs you to find a missing child. That single hook turns a tourist trap into a maze you can't leave — and something is down there beneath the exhibits.

The developers lean hard into environmental storytelling. Instead of cutscenes explaining the plot, you collect tapes, notes, and recordings scattered through the attraction. Community reports from the demo describe piecing together what happened to the staff from fragments rather than exposition dumps.

| Story Element | What It Means for Players |
|---|---|
| Setting: Dalton's Dinos | Crumbling roadside attraction with vertical, climbable terrain |
| Time period: 1992 | No phones or modern tech; isolation is built into the premise |
| Inciting event | A phone call asking you to find a missing child |
| Antagonists | Monstrous creatures that hunt, chase, and torment |
| Narrative delivery | Tapes, notes, and recordings found in the environment |
| Tone | Psychological horror with supernatural and survival elements |

The 1992 framing isn't just flavor. It removes every modern escape option, which means the only solution the game can offer is physical movement. That's a clean piece of design logic, and it explains why parkour is the core mechanic rather than a side activity.

## Gameplay: Horror Meets First-Person Parkour

This is the part that separates Terrible Lizards from the standard hide-and-seek horror formula. The creatures stalking the abandoned dino depot shamble and then charge, and your only tools are movement options. The developers describe the system as intuitive to learn but deep enough to master.

| Move | How It's Used |
|---|---|
| Run | Cover ground fast when a creature locks onto you |
| Jump | Clear gaps between exhibits and broken structures |
| Climb | Scale fences, walls, and attraction props to break line of sight |
| Wall run | Maintain momentum across narrow corridors |
| Bounce | Use surfaces to chain movement and reach higher routes |
| Slide | Duck under obstacles and keep speed through tight spaces |

The emphasis on momentum is deliberate. Fluid movement chains are the difference between escaping and restarting a section, which is why community discussion around the demo focuses so heavily on route memorization rather than combat tricks.

Because there are no weapons, encounters are puzzles with legs. You solve them by reading the environment, choosing a line, and committing. That's a very different skill set from aiming under pressure, and it's the reason the parkour tag sits alongside horror rather than underneath it.

The feature set confirms the direction:

- **Horror plus parkour:** outrun unstoppable creatures using momentum-driven movement.
- **A mystery buried:** explore the attraction and uncover what's underneath it.
- **No weapons:** outsmart and outmaneuver pursuers instead of fighting them.
- **Environmental storytelling:** collect tapes, notes, and recordings to piece together the truth.

If you've played first-person platformers, the learning curve will feel familiar. If you haven't, expect the first hour to be humbling — and expect the chase sequences to be where the tension peaks.
