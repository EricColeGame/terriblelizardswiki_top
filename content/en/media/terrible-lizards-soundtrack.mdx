## Why Dinosaurs Are Called "Terrible Lizards"

The phrase behind the album title is a translation, and a slightly slippery one. In 1842, British anatomist Richard Owen needed a name for the fossil reptiles he and others had been describing, and he built one from Greek.

| Greek root | Literal sense | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| *deinos* | Terrible, fearful, or fearfully great | The "terrible" half of the phrase |
| *sauros* | Lizard | The "lizard" half of the phrase |

The popular rendering is "terrible lizard," though many historians of science argue Owen intended something closer to "fearfully great lizard" — an acknowledgment of size as much as menace. Either way, the name stuck, and it is the reason a modern composer can title a project *Terrible Lizards* and have every listener understand the reference instantly.

It is also worth noting that the name is taxonomically misleading. Dinosaurs are not lizards in the modern sense; they belong to a separate group, [Dinosauria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaur), whose living members are birds. That irony — a "lizard" label applied to animals that are closer to sparrows than to iguanas — is exactly the kind of tension the closing track seems built around.
